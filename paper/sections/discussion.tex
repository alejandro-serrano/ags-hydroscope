\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

Section~\ref{sec:results} described what happened; this section discusses why. Every
configuration was trained once, and no experiment was designed to isolate a cause, so each
explanation below is a hypothesis that fits the evidence, not a proven mechanism. We follow the
research questions: the drop (RQ-b), fine-tuning (RQ-c), the architectures (RQ-a), and the input
bands and weights (RQ-d, RQ-e). We then discuss the Grad-CAM maps, the cost and the limitations.

\subsection{Why the drop was so large (RQ-b)}

We could observe two sources of the drop: the pixels changed, and the classes looked different.
To check the pixels, we compared the median of each band of the April composite with the EuroSAT
training statistics as a z-score, $z = (\tilde{x}_{\mathrm{Ags}} - \mu_{\mathrm{EuroSAT}}) /
\sigma_{\mathrm{EuroSAT}}$. The visible bands (B02--B04) lay \ZVisibleMin{} to \ZVisibleMax{}
standard deviations above the EuroSAT mean and the SWIR bands (B11, B12) \ZSwirMin{} to
\ZSwirMax{} above it, while the near-infrared bands (B08, B8A) stayed within \ZNirMax. This
pattern is consistent with a landscape of bare soil and dry vegetation, which reflect more
visible and SWIR light than the green fields and forests of EuroSAT. Normalization does not
remove this shift, because it applies the same EuroSAT statistics (or, for SSL4EO-S12, the same
fixed scale) to both datasets. In other words, a typical Aguascalientes pixel looked to the
network like an unusually bright EuroSAT pixel.

B10 was the only band that this screening flagged, with $z = \ZBandTen$. B10 is the cirrus band:
it sits in a strong water-vapor absorption band and sees little of the ground, so one possible
reason is the atmosphere, a drier air column over the high plateau or thin cirrus that the cloud
mask missed, rather than a different land cover; we did not test it. Since the screening uses
one median per band for the whole state, it says nothing about individual classes.

The second source is that the same class does not look the same. Fig.~\ref{fig:study_area}(b)
shows it: the EuroSAT Forest patch is a dark, closed canopy, while the Aguascalientes one is a
gray, open cover crossed by tracks. April is the peak of the dry season, so rainfed fields are bare
or fallow, managed grass is brown, and natural cover is xeric scrub on dry, rocky ground, which
resembles neither European forest nor European grassland. This is consistent with
Fig.~\ref{fig:ags_f1}(a), where Forest and Pasture, two classes whose European look depends on
green vegetation, were at or near zero in every run, and with Fig.~\ref{fig:confusion}(a), where
Forest patches went to HerbaceousVegetation. AnnualCrop, whose EuroSAT patches already include
pale, bare parcels (Fig.~\ref{fig:study_area}(b)), kept a nonzero F1 in every run. Some objects
also have no EuroSAT counterpart. The solar park in Fig.~\ref{fig:gradcam}(b), which we labeled
Industrial, looks nothing like the warehouse roofs of EuroSAT, and a model can only answer with
one of the classes it knows.

\subsection{Why fine-tuning helped only in part (RQ-c)}

A likely reason for the small gain of \texttt{head} lies in the features. If the frozen EuroSAT
features had separated the Aguascalientes classes, a new linear classifier trained on 561
labeled patches would likely have found the boundaries. It did not, which suggests that the
differences that matter here, such as canopy density on dry ground or a faint parcel pattern on
bare soil, were not well represented in features learned to separate European classes. A
linear layer can redraw boundaries, but it cannot create a feature the backbone does not
compute.

A second explanation is that the classifier was undertrained. With 561 patches, a batch of 64
and 10 epochs, \texttt{head} took only \FineTuneSteps{} optimizer steps, starting at a learning
rate of $10^{-4}$ that then decayed with the cosine schedule. The training logs agree: in
\HeadLossFallingRuns{} of the \NumRuns{} runs, the \texttt{head} training loss was lower at the
last epoch than at the middle one, so it had not yet leveled off. In other words, a longer
\texttt{head} training might have gained more. Both explanations predict a small gain, so our
runs cannot separate them; a \texttt{head} run trained until its loss stops falling would.

\texttt{last\_block} adapts the high-level features themselves, which is consistent with its
larger gains. Still, the largest part of the drop remained, and we see three likely limits.
First, 561 labels are few, and several classes have a few dozen fine-tune patches or fewer
(Table~\ref{tab:ags_classes}). Second, the 10 fixed epochs had no validation split to choose a
better stopping point. Third, in the two CNNs the frozen layers kept their EuroSAT
BatchNorm statistics, so the spectral shift may still have passed through them before reaching
the trained block. The mode
also has a confound: it trains \LastBlockShareResNet\,\%, \LastBlockShareEffNet\,\% and
\LastBlockShareViT\,\% of the parameters of ResNet-50, EfficientNet-B0 and ViT-S, so the
recovery of different architectures is not a like-for-like comparison.

The ResNet-50/RGB/ImageNet run, where \texttt{last\_block} gave a lower macro-F1 than
\texttt{head}, has two possible explanations. It trained the largest share,
\LastBlockShareResNet\,\% of a model of the size listed in Table~\ref{tab:cost}, on 561 patches,
which leaves room to fit the fine-tune split instead of the region. The class-weighted loss of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:class_weights} also pushed predictions away from HerbaceousVegetation, whose F1 fell
more in this run than in any other (Fig.~\ref{fig:ags_f1}(b)). However, the two 13-band
ResNet-50 runs trained the same share with the same loss and did gain from \texttt{last\_block},
so neither reason explains the run alone. With one seed, we cannot separate them from an unlucky
run.

\subsection{CNNs versus the Vision Transformer (RQ-a)}

On EuroSAT the architecture made little difference (Table~\ref{tab:eurosat}), probably because
the benchmark is close to its ceiling for all three models. In Aguascalientes, among the RGB
ImageNet runs, ViT-S had the highest observed macro-F1 in the zero-shot, \texttt{head} and
\texttt{last\_block} columns of Table~\ref{tab:ags}. The first two columns are free of the
\texttt{last\_block} confound, because there the three models train the same part: nothing, or
only the classifier. With 13 bands and ImageNet weights, however, ResNet-50 came first after
\texttt{last\_block}, so the ViT-S lead is tied to the RGB input in our data.

One hypothesis is global context against local texture. Self-attention relates every token to
every other one, which lets a ViT use long-range relations between pixels~\cite{bazi2021vit},
while a CNN builds its features from local neighborhoods. If the arrangement of a scene, such as
parcel boundaries or a street grid, carried over to Aguascalientes better than its color and
texture, a model that weighs the whole patch would lose less. The Grad-CAM maps are consistent
with this: ViT-S maps spread over the whole patch, while CNN maps cover one compact region. We
cannot confirm the hypothesis, for two reasons besides the single seed
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). Grad-CAM on a ViT, computed on \texttt{norm1} of the last
block, is an approximation, so part of the spread may come from the method. And
\texttt{last\_block} trains only \LastBlockShareViT\,\% of ViT-S. With 561 labels, training
fewer parameters may help or hurt, so this confound could favor either family.

\subsection{RGB versus 13 bands and the pre-trained weights (RQ-d, RQ-e)}

With ImageNet weights, the non-RGB bands entered through filters initialized as the mean of the
RGB filters, so they had no pre-trained meaning. Whatever they learned came from EuroSAT alone,
and the SWIR bands, among the most shifted in our screening, carried that shift straight into the
network. This is consistent with EfficientNet-B0 and ViT-S scoring lower with 13 bands.
ResNet-50 is the exception, and our runs cannot tell why. The answer to RQ-d therefore depends
on the model.

The SSL4EO-S12 weights were learned on all 13 bands, from images sampled worldwide around
populated cities in four seasons~\cite{wang2023ssl4eo}. Every band thus had a pre-trained
meaning, and the pre-training may have included dry landscapes and spectra closer to
Aguascalientes than EuroSAT. This is consistent with the higher macro-F1 of both 13-band
SSL4EO-S12 runs after \texttt{last\_block}. A possible reason why the ViT-S gain appeared only
after \texttt{last\_block}, and not zero-shot, is that EuroSAT training mapped the features to
European classes, and only local training re-mapped them. Two facts keep us from crediting the
weights. The SSL4EO-S12 runs also used another normalization, $x/\num{10000}$, which changes
how the shifted bands reach the first layer. And ViT-S/MS13/SSL4EO was slightly lower than its
ImageNet counterpart on EuroSAT, so the benefit is not uniform.

\subsection{What the Grad-CAM maps suggest}

In Aguascalientes, many maps fall away from the object that defines the class, which is
consistent with models looking for European cues that the patches lack. The solar park of
Fig.~\ref{fig:gradcam}(b) is an out-of-distribution object: no model centered its map on the
panels, and none predicted Industrial. Fig.~\ref{fig:gradcam_selected} adds one
encouraging case: after \texttt{last\_block}, the selected model predicted the AnnualCrop patch
correctly, and its map moved from one corner to a wider part of the fields. One patch, however,
shows a possible behavior, not a general one.

The main error after fine-tuning, HerbaceousVegetation predicted as Forest
(Fig.~\ref{fig:confusion}(b)), has a likely reason in the landscape. The two such patches in
Fig.~\ref{fig:gradcam_selected} show rough ground with dark areas, and one is almost entirely
dark. In Aguascalientes, Forest is oak and pine on the western ranges, dark even in April, so
xeric scrub on rugged hills shares its two most visible cues: dark tone and rough relief. The
guide separates them with a threshold, continuous canopy over 70\,\% of the cell, on what is
in reality a gradual change from scrub to open woodland to forest. The class weights may add
to it: Forest has far fewer fine-tune patches than HerbaceousVegetation
(Table~\ref{tab:ags_classes}), so it gets a much larger weight in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:class_weights}, and borderline patches are pushed toward it.

\subsection{Cost (RQ-a)}

Table~\ref{tab:cost} shows that EfficientNet-B0 has far fewer parameters than the other two
models but a similar time per epoch. We see two possible reasons. First, on the MPS backend the
time may depend more on the number of layers and on moving activations through memory than on
arithmetic, and EfficientNet-B0 has many small layers. Second, the three models may share a
bottleneck in data loading. Every run used the same loader with two worker processes, so if the
loader could not feed the GPU fast enough, all three models would wait about the same time for
data at each step, whatever their size. We did not profile the runs or time the loader on its
own, so we cannot tell the two reasons apart. For the second stage of the project, monthly inference over the whole
state, the useful number is patches/s; there, ViT-S/RGB/ImageNet had the highest observed
throughput, and the 13-band input cost throughput in every model.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

Our results have eight limits. First, each configuration was trained once with seed 0 and no
paired test was run, so small gaps between runs may change with another seed. Second, we used one
month, April 2024, so season and region are mixed in the shift and the rainy season is not
covered. Third, a single annotator labeled the cells, with no agreement measure; the boundaries
between scrub, open woodland and pasture depend on judgment. Fourth, the test set has 541
patches, River only 7, and several classes a few dozen or fewer, so per-class F1 is noisy; skipped
covers, such as greenhouses and quarries, are not measured at all. Fifth, 35 test patches border
a fine-tune cell because the split has no buffer zone, so the fine-tuned scores may be slightly
optimistic. Sixth, the 64-px patches were resized to 224 pixels, which adds no detail and no
context beyond the cell. Seventh, we used L1C data without atmospheric correction, so part of the
spectral shift, as B10 suggests, may be atmospheric. Eighth, peak memory is what the MPS driver
reserved, not what each model needs, and EfficientNet-B0 had no SSL4EO-S12 weights, so RQ-e was
answered for two models only.

\subsection{Future work}

The most direct next step is to train each configuration with several seeds, which would allow a
paired test between models. A buffer zone between fine-tune and test blocks would remove the
adjacency risk, and a fine-tuning mode that trains the same share of parameters in every model
would remove the main confound of the CNN versus ViT comparison. Multi-season composites, which
are part of the planned second stage of the project, would separate the effect of the dry season
from that of the region. More local labels, from a second annotator, would both help fine-tuning
and measure label noise. Finally, other self-supervised Sentinel-2 weights and multimodal input,
such as radar, are worth testing. Radar sees through clouds and measures structure and
moisture, information that the optical bands do not provide.
